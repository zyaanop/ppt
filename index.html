Introduction

What wastewater treatment is

  1. Applying known technology to improve or upgrade the quality of a wastewater.
  2. Wastewater is collected at a central, segregated location — the treatment plant — and subjected to a sequence of processes.
  3. Treatment combines physical, chemical and biological operations to remove solids, organic matter and, often, nutrients.
  4. The degrees of treatment are named in order of increasing intensity: preliminary, primary, secondary, tertiary.
  5. The principal objective is to allow human and industrial effluents to be disposed of without danger to human health and without unacceptable damage to the natural environment.
  6. Remove pathogens — in many countries a disinfection step follows the last treatment stage — and recover what is useful: biogas as fuel, nutrients, and water fit for irrigation.

Section One

Preliminary Treatment

Removing coarse solids and grit — the material that would damage pumps and clog everything downstream.

01
01 / Preliminary06 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 06
Preliminary treatment

Objective and operations

  1. Removal of coarse solids and other large materials often found in raw wastewater.
  2. Typical operations: coarse screening, grit removal, and sometimes comminution of large objects.
  3. In grit chambers the velocity of the water is held high enough — or air is used — to prevent most organic solids from settling.
  4. Comminutors reduce the size of large particles so they can be removed as sludge in later processes.
01 / Preliminary07 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 07
Coarse screening

Screen chamber and bar screens

  1. Influent passes through a bar screen that removes cans, rags, sticks and plastic packets carried in the sewage stream.
  2. Modern plants serving large populations use an automated mechanically raked bar screen.
  3. In smaller or less modern plants a manually cleaned screen is used instead.
01 / Preliminary08 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 08
Grit removal

Grit chamber

  1. A sand or grit channel where the velocity of the incoming sewage is adjusted to allow sand, grit, stones and broken glass to settle.
  2. These particles are removed because they damage pumps and other equipment.
  3. Aerated grit chambers diffuse air to keep organic solids in suspension as the grit settles.
  4. Vortex-type chambers move grit to the outside of the unit where it is collected.
01 / Preliminary09 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 09
Size reduction

Comminutor

  1. All of the wastewater flow passes through the grinder assembly.
  2. The grinder consists of a screen or slotted basket, a rotating or oscillating cutter, and a stationary cutter.
  3. Solids pass through the screen and are chopped or shredded between the two cutters.
  4. Reducing particle size lets them be removed later as sludge rather than as screenings.

Sludge removal

Handling the primary sludge

  1. Settled solids — primary sludge — are removed from the bottom of the tank by sludge rakes that scrape it to a central well.
  2. From that well it is pumped to the sludge processing units.
  3. Scum is swept across the tank surface by water jets or mechanical means and is also pumped to sludge processing.
  4. Primary sedimentation is, in many industrialised countries, the minimum level of pre-application treatment required for wastewater irrigation.

02 / Primary17 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 17
Chemistry then physics

The two steps in detail

STEP 01

Coagulation

Coagulants carrying charges opposite to the suspended solids are added to neutralise the negative charges on dispersed non-settleable solids such as clay and organic substances. Once the charge is neutralised the small suspended particles can stick together, forming microflocs — still too small to see with the naked eye. Common coagulants: alum, aluminium chloride, sodium aluminate, ferric sulphate.

STEP 02

Flocculation

Stirring or agitation at 20–30 rpm for 20–25 minutes encourages the particles to agglomerate into masses large enough to settle or be filtered from the water. This forms macroflocs through an increase in density. Polymers added as coagulation aids bridge, bind and strengthen the floc, adding weight and increasing the settling rate.

02 / Primary18 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 18
The alternative to settling

Dissolved air flotation

  1. Air is dissolved into the water under pressure, then released at atmospheric pressure to form micro-bubbles.
  2. Bubbles attach to suspended solids and lift them to the surface instead of waiting for them to sink.
  3. The float layer is skimmed off as sludge; clarified water is drawn from below.
  4. Systems run with or without recycle — recycling pressurised effluent gives finer, more consistent bubbles.

Section Three

Secondary Treatment

Biological treatment — using microorganisms to metabolise the organic matter that settling cannot touch.

03
03 / Secondary20 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 20
Secondary treatment

Objective

  1. Further treatment of the effluent from primary treatment to remove residual organics and suspended solids.
  2. Aerobic biological treatment is performed in the presence of oxygen by aerobic microorganisms — principally bacteria — that metabolise the organic matter.
  3. This produces more microorganisms and inorganic end products: principally CO₂, NH₃ and H₂O.
  4. Processes differ mainly in how oxygen is supplied and how fast the organisms metabolise the organic matter.

Dispersed-growth reactor

Activated sludge

  1. The reactor is an aeration tank or basin holding a suspension of the wastewater and microorganisms — the mixed liquor.
  2. Contents are mixed vigorously by aeration devices, which also supply the oxygen the biological suspension needs.
  3. Devices used include submerged diffusers that release compressed air and mechanical surface aerators that agitate the liquid surface.
  4. Hydraulic retention time is usually 3 to 8 hours, and can be higher with high-BOD5 wastewaters.
  5. After aeration the microorganisms are separated by sedimentation and the clarified liquid is secondary effluent.
  6. A portion of the sludge is recycled to hold the mixed-liquor suspended solids (MLSS) level; the remainder is wasted, keeping the microorganism concentration constant.

Fixed-film treatment

Trickling filters

  1. A basin or tower filled with support media such as stones, plastic shapes or wooden slats.
  2. Wastewater is applied intermittently, or sometimes continuously, over the media.
  3. Microorganisms attach to the media and form a biological layer — the fixed film or biofilm.
  4. Organic matter in the wastewater diffuses into the film, where it is metabolised.
  5. Oxygen is supplied by the natural flow of air, either up or down through the media, depending on the relative temperatures of the wastewater and ambient air.

Waste Water TreatmentSheet 26
RBC

Rotating biological contactors

  1. Fixed-film reactors similar to biofilters — organisms are attached to support media.
  2. In an RBC the support media are slowly rotating discs, partially submerged in flowing wastewater.
  3. Oxygen reaches the attached biofilm from the air when the film is out of the water, and from the liquid when submerged, since oxygen is transferred to the wastewater by surface turbulence created by the discs' rotation.
  4. Sloughed pieces of biofilm are removed in the same way as for biofilters.

Film renewal · where fixed film wins

Sloughing and biofiltration

  1. The biofilm thickens as new organisms grow, and portions periodically slough off the media. The sloughed material is settled out in a secondary clarifier; a portion of the clarified liquid is often recycled to improve hydraulic distribution over the filter.
  2. Biofiltration is a fixed-film process: a bed of media on which microorganisms attach and grow to form biofilm.
  3. It treats wastewater from a wide range of sources, with varying organic compositions and concentrations.
  4. It is versatile — adaptable to small flows (<1 m³/d) as well as municipal flows (>240 000 m³/d).
  5. RBCs have high BOD removal efficiency, low power consumption, low space requirement and low sludge production.

Section Four

Waste Stabilization Ponds

Natural treatment driven by sunlight, algae and time — no aerators, no energy input.

04
04 / Stabilization Ponds32 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 32
Definition

What a stabilization pond is

  1. WSPs are among the main natural wastewater treatment methods.
  2. They are man-made earthen basins, comprising at any one location one or more series of anaerobic, facultative and — depending on the effluent quality required — maturation ponds.
  3. Stabilization is the process by which the organic content and pathogens are reduced.
  4. WSPs are particularly suited to tropical and subtropical countries, since sunlight and ambient temperature are the key factors in their performance.
  5. They reduce organic content, measured as BOD, and kill pathogens. After treatment the effluent may return to surface water, or be reused for irrigation if quality allows.
  6. True waste stabilization ponds use no aerators — high-performance lagoon technology with aerators has far more in common with activated sludge.
04 / Stabilization Ponds33 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 33
Three pond types in series

Types of WSP

01

Anaerobic / Aerobic pond

Primary treatment is carried out in anaerobic ponds. Aerobic ponds reduce organic content measured as BOD and kill pathogens in the wastewater.

02

Facultative pond

Secondary treatment. Removes organic matter — normally expressed as BOD — together with Vibrio cholerae and helminth eggs.

03

Maturation pond

Tertiary treatment. Removes faecal viruses such as rotavirus and norovirus, faecal bacteria such as Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter and pathogenic E. coli, and nutrients.

Oxidation pond chemistry

The reactions

An oxidation pond is a low-cost aerobic stabilization pond. Sewage is stabilised in the presence of bacteria and algae on the principle of symbiosis.

Reaction I — bacterial oxidationSewage + O2  —— bacteria ——▸  CO2 + H2O
Reaction II — algal photosynthesisCO2 + nutrients  —— sunlight + algae ——▸  O2
  1. Organic matter is degraded by the algae aerobically, and CO₂ is released.
  2. That CO₂ is consumed by the algae — microscopic green plants — for photosynthesis in the presence of sunlight.
  3. The remaining oxygen demand of the bacteria is met by taking atmospheric oxygen dissolved in water.
  4. The carbon is used to produce more carbohydrates, and the released oxygen keeps the dissolved oxygen content of the water high.

04 / Stabilization Ponds37 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 37
The other two

Maturation ponds and aerated lagoons

A

Maturation pond

A self-purifying reservoir used to stabilize effluent from mechanized secondary treatment. Receiving wastes with low BOD, it may be clear enough for pathogen reduction through exposure to UV solar radiation. It must be shallow with a large surface area so oxygen dissolves readily and light penetrates the full depth. It can be combined with a rainwater reservoir to form an ecological swimming pond, and adding fish controls the bug issues naturally.

B

Aerated lagoon

A pond with artificial aeration to promote the biological oxidation of wastewaters. This is where pond technology stops being passive: once aerators are added, the system behaves far more like activated sludge than like a true waste stabilization pond.

Advantages and disadvantages

Oxidation ponds weighed up

Advantages

  • Successful in the disposal of sewage
  • Help in evaporation of some liquid and percolation of the remaining portion

  • Provide suitable contents for industrial use

Disadvantages

Section Five

Anaerobic Bioreactors

Treatment in the absence of free dissolved oxygen — where the by-product is fuel, not waste.

05
05 / Anaerobic Bioreactors40 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 40
Principle

Anaerobic treatment of wastewater

  1. Anaerobic bacteria transform organic matter in the wastewater into biogas, containing large amounts of methane and carbon dioxide.
  2. It occurs in the absence of free dissolved oxygen — the organisms survive by using bound molecular oxygen in compounds present in wastewater, such as nitrates and sulphates.
  3. It is an energy-efficient process: most of the organic carbon becomes biogas, and little goes to sludge production.
  4. Often used for industrial wastewater with high organic loads in warm temperatures, or as a pretreatment prior to aerobic municipal treatment.
  5. A foul smell is evolved during the process.

05 / Anaerobic Bioreactors43 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 43
Breaking things down

Stages one and two

STAGE 01

Hydrolysis

Large organic compounds are converted into simple monomeric compounds. It is accomplished through extracellular enzymes. Cellulose is converted to starch using cellulases; casein to amino acids using proteases; triglycerides to fatty acids using lipases. Protein becomes amino acid, hydrogen and acetate.

STAGE 02

Acidogenesis

The soluble monomeric compounds formed by hydrolysis undergo fermentation. Products are propionic acid and butyric acid, along with intermediates such as acetic acid, ethanol, hydrogen and carbon dioxide. Bacteria that bring about fermentation include Lactobacillus sp. In this stage, acid production causes the pH to fall.

05 / Anaerobic Bioreactors44 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 44
Making methane

Stages three and four

STAGE 03

Acetogenesis

Some acetate is produced through mixed acid fermentation. The rest is produced through secondary fermentation of the products obtained in the previous stage. Acetate formation is important for the formation of methane. Example of an acetogenic bacterium: Acetobacter sp.

STAGE 04

Methanogenesis

The last metabolic stage. Methane is formed either from acetate or from carbon dioxide and hydrogen. The bacteria responsible are called methanogens: litotrophic or hydrogenotrophic produce methane from CO₂ and H₂; acetotrophs or acetoclastic convert acetate to methane; methylotrophs convert methanol to methane.

05 / Anaerobic Bioreactors45 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 45
Temperature · pH · mixing

Factors affecting sludge digestion

  1. Temperature. Most methane-forming bacteria are active in two ranges: the mesophilic range, 30 to 35 °C, and the thermophilic range, 50 to 60 °C. Between 40 and 50 °C, ethane-forming bacteria are inhibited.
  2. pH. When digester pH is 7.2 or lower, the presence of NH4+ is favoured; above 7.2, NH3 is favoured. Dissolved ammonia gas is toxic to bacteria, especially methane-forming bacteria.
  3. Mixing. Mixing enhances digestion by distributing bacteria, substrate and nutrients throughout the digester and by equalising temperature. But methane-forming bacteria are very sensitive to rapid mixing — rapid mixing continuously washes them out in the effluent.

Working principle

UASB zones and components

  1. Inlet zone. Provides uniform distribution of the influent across the base of the reactor.
  2. Sludge blanket / reaction zone. Holds the granular biomass and allows the biochemical reactions to take place. Solids are retained by the filtration effect of the blanket.
  3. Settling zone / clarifier zone. Provides the opportunity for solids settling before the effluent leaves.
  4. Gas–liquid–solid separator (GLSS). Separates the three phases: gas to a collection pipe, solids pushed back to the settling zone, water on to the effluent.
  5. The upflow regime and the motion of the gas bubbles mix the reactor without any mechanical assistance.

Advantages and disadvantages

UASB weighed up

Advantages

  • High reduction of BOD
  • Withstands high organic and hydraulic loading rates
  • Low sludge production — infrequent desludging
  • Biogas usable for energy, though usually first requires scrubbing
  • No aeration system required, so very little energy consumption
  • Effluent rich in nutrients, usable for agricultural irrigation
  • Low land demand — can be constructed underground

Disadvantages

  • May be unstable with variable hydraulic and organic loads
  • Requires operation and maintenance by skilled personnel
  • Long start-up time to reach full capacity
  • A constant source of electricity is required
  • Not all parts and materials may be locally available
  • Effluent and sludge require further treatment or appropriate discharge
  • Not adapted for cold regions

Fixed bed of catalyst or biocatalyst

Packed bed reactor

  1. A cylinder of large diameter with multiple catalyst beds, or many tubes in parallel packed with catalyst and encased in a large shell.
  2. Packing provides good contact between the contacting phases; the chemical reaction takes place on the surface of the catalyst.
  3. Packed bed bioreactors are commonly used for attached biofilms in wastewater engineering — the immobilised biocatalyst is fed with nutrients from the top or bottom.
  4. The advantage is higher conversion per weight of catalyst than other catalytic reactors.
  5. The disadvantage is changed flow characteristics: bed compaction and channelling alter the bed porosity during operation.

Section Six

Tertiary & Advanced Treatment

Everything secondary treatment leaves behind — nutrients, pathogens and residual solids.

06
06 / Tertiary & Advanced53 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 53
Tertiary / advanced treatment

When and why

  1. Employed when specific wastewater constituents cannot be removed by secondary treatment.
  2. Because advanced treatment usually follows high-rate secondary treatment, it is sometimes referred to as tertiary treatment.
  3. But advanced processes are sometimes combined with primary or secondary treatment — chemical addition to primary clarifiers or aeration basins to remove phosphorus — or used in place of secondary treatment, as in overland flow treatment of primary effluent.
  4. The four objectives: removal of suspended solids, removal of dissolved solids, removal of nitrate and phosphate, and killing of microorganisms.
06 / Tertiary & Advanced54 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 54
Objectives one and two

Removing what is left

i

Removal of suspended solids

Microstraining — sewage is placed in a rotating drum filter and the drum is rotated; during rotation, clear water comes out of the drum and suspended solids remain inside. Chemical coagulation and filtration — precipitating agents such as alum are added; fine suspended solids adsorb to the surface of the Al(OH)₃ precipitate, and the precipitate with adsorbed solids is separated by filtration.

ii

Removal of dissolved solids

Adsorption by activated carbon — dissolved solids are removed by filtering the water through a filter containing activated carbon particles. Reverse osmosis — removes dissolved solids such as NaCl, along with microbial cells.

iii · Nitrate and phosphate

Nitrification and denitrification

  1. Eutrophication is the process by which a body of water becomes overly enriched with minerals and nutrients, inducing excessive growth of algae.
  2. Nitrification is an aerobic process transforming ammonium nitrogen — the reduced state — into an oxidised form, NO₃⁻. It is carried out by autotrophic bacteria of the genera Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter. Overall reaction: NH4+ + 2O2 → NO3− + 2H+ + H2O.
  3. Denitrification removes the nitrate formed during nitrification. It requires an anoxic environment — void of dissolved oxygen. Denitrifying heterotrophic microorganisms such as Thiobacillus denitrificans and Micrococcus denitrificans reduce nitrate into nitrogen gas, which is released into the atmosphere.
  4. Phosphate is removed by bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Bacillus by the microbial absorption process.
06 / Tertiary & Advanced57 / 60
Waste Water TreatmentSheet 57
EBPR · iv · Killing microorganisms

Phosphorus removal and disinfection

EBPR

Enhanced biological phosphorus removal

Biological phosphorus removal is accomplished by providing alternating anaerobic and aerobic treatment of the wastewater. Such systems promote the growth of phosphorus accumulating organisms (PAOs) such as Acinetobacter. PAOs take up an excess amount of phosphorus from the wastewater during the aerobic phase of biological treatment. Phosphorus is then removed from the system with the waste activated sludge.

iv

Killing of microorganisms

Finally, microorganisms in sewage are killed by disinfection. Chemical agents — chlorine is most commonly used; bromine, iodine, ozone, alcohols, phenols, heavy metals, hydrogen peroxide, alkalies and acids are sometimes employed. Physical agents — heat and light, including ultra-violet rays. Radiation agents — gamma rays.

Waste Water TreatmentSheet 58
Merits and disadvantages

Anaerobic treatment in the round

Advantages

  • High degree of waste stabilization
  • Low production of excess biological sludge, which can be dried directly on a sludge drying bed without further treatment
  • Low nutrition requirements
  • No oxygen requirement
  • Production of a valuable by-product: methane gas
  • Organic loading is not limited
  • Less land requirement
  • Non-feed conditions for a few months do not affect the system adversely — it can work seasonally

Disadvantages

  • Incomplete break-down of organic compounds
  • No thorough nutrient removal
  • Later aerobic purification with nutrient removal is often still needed
  • Most efficient in the mesophilic range, 30–37 °C, so the influent must be heated in most cases
  • Less robust with regard to toxicity and inhibition
  • Risk of odour problems
